How the planning horizon changes optimal control

A worked tutorial: solve a scalar linear-quadratic control problem with the calculus of variations, step by step, then see how the solution changes with the horizon \(T\).

The problem

\[ \min_{u(\cdot)}\; J=\frac12\int_0^T\big(q\,x(t)^2+r\,u(t)^2\big)\,dt \quad\text{s.t.}\quad \dot x=a\,x+b\,u,\quad x(0)=x_0,\quad x(T)\ \text{free}. \]

The state \(x\) is the distance from a target at 0, and the control \(u\) is how hard we push toward it. Left alone, the state grows if \(a\gt0\), decays if \(a\lt0\) and stays put if \(a=0\); \(b\neq0\) sets how strongly the control acts. The weight \(q\gt0\) charges for being away from the target and \(r\gt0\) charges for effort. The two terms pull in opposite directions, and the optimum balances them over the horizon \([0,T]\).

Two details shape everything below: the final state \(x(T)\) is free, and the cost has no terminal term. Nothing after \(T\) enters the problem.

Solving it with the calculus of variations

The plan: rewrite the problem as a minimization over paths \(x(\cdot)\), require the first variation to vanish, and read off a differential equation with boundary conditions. Then solve it.

1Eliminate the control

The dynamics are a constraint linking \(u\) and \(x\), not a control law. Because \(b\neq0\), they can be solved for \(u\), so each path \(x(\cdot)\) fixes its control and we can optimize over paths directly.

From \(\dot x=ax+bu\), the control along a path is \(u=(\dot x-ax)/b\). Substituting into the cost gives

\[ J[x]=\int_0^T L(x,\dot x)\,dt,\qquad L(x,\dot x)=\frac12\Big(q\,x^2+\frac{r}{b^2}\big(\dot x-a x\big)^2\Big). \]

\(J\) is a functional: it maps a whole function \(x(\cdot)\) to a number. We look for the \(x(\cdot)\) with \(x(0)=x_0\) that makes it smallest.

2Perturb the path

At a minimizer, any small admissible change of the path must leave the cost unchanged to first order, just as \(f'(x^*)=0\) for an ordinary function.

Take a candidate \(x\) and a perturbation \(\eta(t)\). Because \(x(0)=x_0\) is fixed, we need \(\eta(0)=0\); because \(x(T)\) is free, \(\eta(T)\) is unrestricted. Define \(\phi(\varepsilon)=J[x+\varepsilon\eta]\). The first variation is

\[ \delta J=\phi'(0)=\int_0^T\Big(\frac{\partial L}{\partial x}\,\eta+\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot x}\,\dot\eta\Big)dt. \]

For our \(L\), writing \(u=(\dot x-ax)/b\) to keep the expressions short,

\[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial x}=q\,x-\frac{ra}{b^2}\big(\dot x-ax\big)=q\,x-\frac{ra}{b}\,u,\qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot x}=\frac{r}{b^2}\big(\dot x-ax\big)=\frac{r}{b}\,u. \]

3Integrate by parts

The term with \(\dot\eta\) is awkward because \(\eta\) and \(\dot\eta\) are not independent. Moving the derivative off \(\eta\) leaves everything multiplied by \(\eta\) itself.

\[ \delta J=\Big[\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot x}\,\eta\Big]_0^T+\int_0^T\Big(\frac{\partial L}{\partial x}-\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot x}\Big)\eta\,dt. \]

4Set the first variation to zero

\(\delta J=0\) must hold for every admissible \(\eta\). Choosing \(\eta\) cleverly splits this into an equation in the interior and conditions at the ends.

Interior. First take perturbations with \(\eta(0)=\eta(T)=0\). The boundary term vanishes, and by the fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations the integrand must vanish. This is the Euler–Lagrange equation:

\[ q\,x-\frac{ra}{b^2}\big(\dot x-ax\big)-\frac{r}{b^2}\big(\ddot x-a\dot x\big)=0. \]

Multiply by \(b^2/r\). The two \(a\dot x\) terms cancel, leaving

\[ \ddot x=\omega^2x,\qquad \omega=\sqrt{a^2+\frac{q\,b^2}{r}}. \]

Start. \(\eta(0)=0\), so the boundary term at \(t=0\) is always zero. The condition there is \(x(0)=x_0\).

End. What remains is \(\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot x}(T)\,\eta(T)=\frac{r}{b}\,u(T)\,\eta(T)=0\). Since \(\eta(T)\) can be anything, we need \(u(T)=0\), which in terms of the state reads \(\dot x(T)=a\,x(T)\).

This end condition is a natural (or transversality) boundary condition. It fixes the final control, \(u(T)=0\), and leaves the final state free. In the final instant, effort buys nothing, because no time is left for it to reduce the state cost. A fixed endpoint \(x(T)=0\) would force \(\eta(T)=0\), and this condition would be replaced by \(x(T)=0\).

Together, we have a two-point boundary-value problem:

\[ \ddot x=\omega^2x,\qquad x(0)=x_0,\qquad \dot x(T)=a\,x(T). \]

5Solve the boundary-value problem

Writing the general solution in terms of the time-to-go \(\tau=T-t\) makes the end condition easy to apply.

The general solution is \(x=A\cosh\omega\tau+B\sinh\omega\tau\), so \(\dot x=-A\omega\sinh\omega\tau-B\omega\cosh\omega\tau\). At \(t=T\) (\(\tau=0\)) the end condition reads \(-B\omega=aA\), so \(B=-aA/\omega\). Define

\[ D(\tau)=\cosh\omega\tau-\frac{a}{\omega}\sinh\omega\tau, \]

which is positive because \(|a|\lt\omega\). Then \(x(0)=A\,D(T)=x_0\), and \(u=(\dot x-ax)/b\) simplifies using \(\omega^2-a^2=qb^2/r\):

\[ x^*(t)=x_0\,\frac{D(T-t)}{D(T)},\qquad u^*(t)=-x_0\,\frac{q\,b}{r\,\omega}\,\frac{\sinh\omega(T-t)}{D(T)}. \]

With \(a=0\) and \(b=q=r=1\), this reduces to \(x^*=x_0\cosh(T-t)/\cosh T\) and \(u^*=-x_0\sinh(T-t)/\cosh T\).

6Confirm it is a minimum and compute the cost

\(\delta J=0\) identifies stationary paths. Convexity makes a stationary path globally optimal.

\(L\) is a sum of squares of linear functions of \((x,\dot x)\), so it is convex, and any stationary path is a global minimizer. Because \(J\) is quadratic, every admissible perturbation \(\eta\) gives

\[ J[x^*+\eta]=J[x^*]+\underbrace{\delta J}_{=\,0}+\frac12\int_0^T\Big(q\,\eta^2+\frac{r}{b^2}\big(\dot\eta-a\eta\big)^2\Big)dt\;\ge\;J[x^*]. \]

For the optimal cost, use that \(L\) is quadratic in \((x,\dot x)\), so \(x\,\partial_xL+\dot x\,\partial_{\dot x}L=2L\). Integrating the second term by parts and using the Euler–Lagrange equation and \(u^*(T)=0\):

\[ J^*=\frac12\Big[x\,\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot x}\Big]_0^T=-\frac{r}{2b}\,x_0\,u^*(0)=\frac12\,\frac{q\tanh\omega T}{\omega-a\tanh\omega T}\,x_0^2. \]

7Read off a feedback law

The solution was computed for one particular \(x_0\). Dividing \(u^*\) by \(x^*\) removes \(x_0\) and leaves a rule that depends only on time.

\[ u^*=-\frac{b}{r}\,P(t)\,x,\qquad P(t)=\frac{q\tanh\omega(T-t)}{\omega-a\tanh\omega(T-t)}. \]

So the optimal control is linear state feedback with a gain that depends on the time-to-go \(T-t\). \(P\) rises with time-to-go toward \(P_\infty=r(a+\omega)/b^2\), falls to \(P(T)=0\) at the end, which is the natural boundary condition from step 4 in feedback form, and gives the optimal cost \(J^*=\tfrac12P(0)\,x_0^2\).

As \(T\to\infty\), the gain is constant, \(u=-\tfrac{a+\omega}{b}\,x\), and the closed loop is \(\dot x=-\omega x\). The infinite-horizon controller makes the state decay at rate \(\omega\) whatever \(a\) is, including an unstable plant with \(a\gt0\).

Connection to the Riccati equation. The same gain comes out of dynamic programming. With the value function \(V(t,x)=\tfrac12P(t)x^2\), the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation reduces to \(-\dot P=q+2aP-\tfrac{b^2}{r}P^2\) with \(P(T)=0\), whose solution is the \(P(t)\) above; its stationary version \(0=q+2aP-\tfrac{b^2}{r}P^2\) gives \(P_\infty\). The calculus of variations gives the best path from one initial state; the Riccati equation gives the best rule from every state, and for this problem the two coincide.

With a terminal cost

Adding a terminal cost \(\tfrac12 s\,x(T)^2\) with \(s\ge0\) to \(J\) leaves the Euler–Lagrange equation \(\ddot x=\omega^2x\) unchanged and modifies only the end condition, which becomes \(\tfrac{r}{b}\,u(T)+s\,x(T)=0\). The optimal policy is again linear state feedback, \(u^*=-\tfrac{b}{r}P(t)\,x\), with

\[ P(t)=\frac{s\,\omega+(q+a s)\tanh\omega\tau}{\omega+\big(\tfrac{s b^2}{r}-a\big)\tanh\omega\tau},\qquad \tau=T-t. \]

This \(P\) solves the same Riccati equation with \(P(T)=s\), and the optimal cost is still \(J^*=\tfrac12P(0)\,x_0^2\). As a function of time-to-go, \(P\) moves monotonically from \(s\) at the end toward \(P_\infty\):

  • \(s=0\) recovers the gain derived above, which falls to zero at \(T\).
  • \(0\lt s\lt P_\infty\): the gain dips toward \(s\) at the end. \(s\gt P_\infty\): it rises toward \(s\), and the controller pushes harder late.
  • \(s=P_\infty\): \(P(t)\equiv P_\infty\) for every horizon. The terminal cost equals the infinite-horizon cost-to-go, so the finite-horizon controller is the infinite-horizon one.
  • \(s\to\infty\): the gain grows without bound as \(t\to T\) and forces \(x(T)=0\), the fixed-endpoint problem.

Takeaways

  • The dynamics \(\dot x=ax+bu\) are a constraint. Solving them for \(u\) turns the problem into minimizing a functional \(J[x]\) over paths.
  • The first variation gives the Euler–Lagrange equation \(\ddot x=\omega^2x\) in the interior and conditions at the ends: a fixed start gives \(x(0)=x_0\), a free end gives the natural condition \(u(T)=0\).
  • Convexity of \(L\) makes the stationary path the global minimizer.
  • Dividing \(u^*\) by \(x^*\) reveals a feedback law whose gain \(P(t)\) depends only on time-to-go and solves the Riccati equation.
  • \(\omega=\sqrt{a^2+qb^2/r}\) sets the time scale. Over long horizons the early behaviour approaches the infinite-horizon law, which gives decay rate \(\omega\), but the gain still falls to zero at \(T\) because nothing is charged after it.
  • A terminal cost \(\tfrac12P_\infty x(T)^2\) removes the end effect: the finite-horizon gain is then constant and equal to \(P_\infty\).
